Do countries that educate more girls have smaller families?

Using World Bank indicators from 1970 to 2019, we compare how many girls are enrolled in school with how many children women have, country by country and year by year.

Between 1990 and 2015, countries typically see more girls in school with fewer births per woman

Each dot is one country that reports both measures in that year. Area is proportional to population; color shows whether girls (blue) or boys (red) have higher secondary enrollment. Gross enrollment can exceed 100% when students outside the official age range are enrolled. Aggregates such as “World” or income groups were removed.

Write-up

Rationale for Design Decisions

Each group member worked with the same World Bank data folders but developed a different question and interactive visualization. The alternatives included a scatter plot comparing female and male life expectancy with the share of the population aged 65 and older, a “Who Outlives Whom?” visualization showing the gender gap in life expectancy, a life-expectancy trend chart with country selection and zoom, an age-dependency visualization, a regional fertility trend chart, and a visualization relating fertility to an aging population.

As a group, we compared the different ideas based on two main criteria: how interesting and understandable the question was, and how effectively interaction could help the reader explore the data. Several of the individual visualizations showed meaningful patterns, but we ultimately decided to focus the final application on the question: Do countries that educate more girls have smaller families?

We chose a scatter plot because the final question compares two quantitative variables directly. This is typically a common, general graphic that most people have seen before and can understand, helping make the information easier to digest. Girls’ secondary-school enrollment is placed on the horizontal axis, and fertility rate is placed on the vertical axis, making it easy to see whether countries with higher girls’ enrollment also tend to have lower fertility. Our data transformation step included filtering out ‘World’ and income group aggregates which would likely also be factors in the information provided by the graphs, but is not the focus of the question or information being explored. This data was ignored from the ‘health.csv’ but we ensured our webpage had this information portrayed in the subtitle of the static graphs. Each circle represents a country. Circle size reflects population so differences in country size are visible, while color shows the gap between girls’ and boys’ enrollment. These two encodings help show differences in countries on the graph while also clearly depicting patterns between countries higher up to the left compared to countries lower on the right. While size is an important indicator of the number of expected women in a country, the color provides a purposeful way to morally express the importance of women’s education where red which is typically an alarming, flag raising color shows the level of improvement and change still needed in our world. Users can also filter on highlighting specific countries either by clicking on them in the interactive plot or using the search filtering to specify the countries they’re most interested in exploring. By default, ‘West Bank and Gaze’ and ‘United States’ are pinned as the highlighted countries. These highlighted countries also have a persisting trend line that their circle follows from the start of the countries reported data to its end. There is also a dropdown option to change the education information from secondary schooling of girls to university education of women by these countries that explore a deeper and connected relationship between women’s education and family burdens.

We decided that an interactive year slider would make the visualization more engaging and help show how the relationship changes over time to have a baseline of the accepted replacement level. Users can press Play or move the slider themselves, hover over circles for details, search for and pin countries, and follow individual countries across different years. Keeping the axes fixed makes movement from one year to another easier to compare. The application also provides a yearly summary and a correlation chart so users can examine how strongly girls’ enrollment and fertility are related over time while being reminded that correlation does not establish causation. As the reader plays with the slider, there is a helpful box that outlines what the viewer is seeing from the interactive chart, that provides a comparison between the top and bottom third of countries in girl’s education.

We considered keeping multiple separate visualizations because each explored a different aspect of the World Bank data. However, that approach made the project feel more like a collection of charts than one connected story. We ultimately chose the education-and-fertility question because it gave the project a clearer narrative and allowed several interaction techniques to support the same question. Instead of only showing that fertility has fallen over time, the final visualization lets the reader explore whether that decline occurred alongside increases in girls’ education at the country level.
